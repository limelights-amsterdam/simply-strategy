# The seven patterns, and the tone

Read this when you rewrite a text or when a sentence feels vague and you cannot say why.

## The seven patterns that make text vague

This is the core. Each pattern has a name, so you can point at it and replace it. Walk them before
you send anything.

<!-- plainlint-ignore-start -->

**1. Synonym rotation.** The same thing gets three names in one paragraph. The reader cannot tell
whether it is one thing or three.
- Pick one name per thing and keep it for the whole document. Even when it gets boring. Boring is good here.

**2. Stacked hedging.** Auxiliary verbs pile up until nothing happens.
- ❌ It is important to note that this could potentially help improve performance.
- ✅ This makes it faster. (Or, if you do not know: *I expect this is faster, but I have not measured it.*)
- Uncertainty is allowed. Name it once, concretely. Not five times, vaguely.

**3. Frozen verbs.** A verb disguised as a noun, with an empty verb in front of it.
- ❌ perform an analysis of / provide assistance → ✅ analyze / help
- If the action sits in the noun, put it back in the verb.

**4. Marketing adjectives.** Words that *claim* quality instead of *showing* it.
- ❌ seamless, powerful, groundbreaking, industry-leading, effortless, robust, next level, cutting-edge
- ✅ Replace with the fact the claim rests on. Not "seamless integration" but "you do not have to change any code".
- If you cannot measure it or point at it, cut it.

**5. Run-on sentences.** Four ideas joined by dashes and semicolons where four sentences belonged.
- One idea per sentence. Split on the dash.

**6. Office speak and vague verbs.** Words that sound like work but point at nothing.
- ❌ reach out, dive into, spin up, circle back, leverage, take a look at, socialize, align on
- ✅ Say the action: delete, send, call, install, test.
- Ask yourself "what does someone literally do here?" and write that down.

**7. Abstract instruction.** The sentence is correct but does not say what anyone should do. This is
the only pattern about content rather than form, and usually the most expensive.
- ❌ Exercise due care at all times so as not to endanger yourself or others.
- ✅ Watch out for pedestrians, obstacles and other vehicles.
- An instruction with no visible action is not finished. Research on jury instructions found
  comprehension rose 47% through exactly this conversion.

Also avoid: everything in threes ("faster, smarter, stronger"), the inversion ("it's not X, it's Y"),
and the rhetorical question as a heading.

## The tone

The rules above make text clear. These make it human. Without this part you get a manual where a
conversation belonged.

- **Write to the reader, not about them.** "You", not "the user" or "one".
- **Everyday speech is fine.** Contractions: *don't*, *you're*.
- **Present tense.** "The button opens the menu", not "will open".
- **Active.** Make it visible who acts. Passive only when the actor is genuinely unknown.
- **Vary your sentence length.** This is the most important anti-robot rule. The limit is a ceiling,
  not a target. Every sentence at 18 words reads like a metronome. Put a four-word sentence next to a
  twenty-word one. That is how you hear a person.
- **Friendly, not silly.** No exclamation marks, no jokes that slow the explanation down.
- **Condition before instruction.** "If you are on macOS, use X." Not the other way round, or someone
  reads a step that does not apply to them.
