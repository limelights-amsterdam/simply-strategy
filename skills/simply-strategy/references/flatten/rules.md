# The five moves, and how each one fails

## 1. Every sentence under 15 words

Split, do not compress. A 30-word sentence becomes two sentences of 14, not one sentence of 15 with
half the meaning removed.

**Fails when** you start deleting clauses to hit the count. Watch for a sentence that got shorter and
also got vaguer. That is not flattening, that is loss.

**Check:** count the words in the longest sentence. If it is 16, fix it. There is no rounding here.

## 2. Every abstract noun becomes a person doing something

Ask: who does what to whom? Then write that.

Three questions find the person fastest. They come from the sacrifice test in
`references/substance/tests.md`, and they work because each one has a face in it:

- Which customer calls next year and gets no?
- Who walks into the house, or picks up the phone, once this is done?
- Who inside the organisation gets angry about this? If nobody does, nothing was chosen, and that
  is the finding.

| Abstract | Person doing something |
|---|---|
| the transformation journey | we stop selling to shops and start selling online |
| organisational readiness | whether our people can actually do this |
| customer-centricity | we ask customers before we build |
| operational excellence | fewer mistakes, faster delivery |
| strategic alignment | the four directors agree on the same thing |
| value creation | we make more money, or the customer saves time |

**Fails when** you cannot name the person, because the original never had one. Then you have found
something real. Write `[TO FILL: who]` rather than inventing an actor.

## 3. Every number gets a comparison

A percentage on its own is a decoration. Turn it into something countable.

- 12% → of every 100, 12
- 3x growth → three times as much as now
- €4.2m opportunity → about what we make in a normal month [TO FILL: confirm monthly revenue]
- 40 basis points → four cents on every ten euros

**Fails when** the comparison needs a number the document does not have. Flag it. Never estimate a
denominator to make a comparison work, that is inventing a number with extra steps.

## 4. Every recommendation gets its sacrifice

"We do X" is not finished until "which means we stop doing Y" sits next to it.

**Fails when** the document genuinely has no sacrifice in it. Do not invent one. Write the
recommendation, then write: *the plan does not say what this costs.* That line is worth more than
anything you could have made up.

## 5. Nothing survives the opposite test

Reverse the claim. Would a sensible organisation claim the reverse?

| Claim | Reversed | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| We put the customer first | We put the customer last | Empty |
| We invest in quality | We do not invest in quality | Empty |
| We serve only companies above 200 staff | We also serve small business | Real choice |
| We do not sell through resellers | We do sell through resellers | Real choice |

**Fails when** you cut an empty claim and leave a gap. An empty claim usually sat where a real choice
should have been. Say that: *the plan says it puts customers first. It does not say which customers
it will not serve.*

---

## 6. One name per thing, and none of the run's own words

The page calls the material **the plan**, from the first line to the last. Not "this vision" in one
paragraph and "the document" in the next, whatever the material calls itself. The same for every
recurring thing: one verb for what the plan does to money ("taking money back", not also "reclaims"),
one name for each organisation.

The run has words of its own that never reach the page: compass, angle, must-solve, flatten level,
pointer, TO FILL. A gap is a sentence ("Nobody has said who builds it"), a source is a superscript,
the level lives in the log. The reader of the page does not know what a compass field is and does
not need to.

Words that sound plain to the writer and are not to a board, with what to write instead:

| Not this | This |
|---|---|
| award, awarded | payment, paid, benefit decided |
| scope | what it is for, what it keeps |
| enforcement | checking for misuse |
| baseline | a starting number |
| in-house | itself |
| indispensable | cannot do without |
| legal route, legal basis | a law behind it, who changes the law |
| stakeholder | the name of the person or body |

**No fragments, no colon as a verb.** "Systems due now." is a note. "The systems are due now." is a
sentence. "The heaviest bet: the Belastingdienst stays." hides the verb; "The biggest bet is on the
Belastingdienst. It must stay until the new process works." has two.

**Your own reading is labelled, in four words.** "That is our reading." at the end of the sentence,
not a clause about what the plan does not state.

**Fails when** a term from the material survives because it was in the material. The material's
word for a thing is evidence of what it says, not a reason to keep it on a page meant for people who
never read the material.

---

## The order

Substance, then language, then de-slop. Always.

Rewriting an empty sentence gives you a tidy empty sentence. If move 5 kills half the document, that
is the finding, and it goes in section 6 of the output rather than being quietly smoothed over.
